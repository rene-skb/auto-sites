# Objection Handling — Cold Outreach to Local Businesses

*For Scott's in-person and follow-up conversations. Not scripts — frameworks. Sound like yourself.*

---

## "I don't need a website — my regulars find me fine"

**What they're really saying:** I'm surviving without one, so why spend money?

**Your angle:** You're not trying to keep your regulars. You're trying to catch everyone who *almost* came in but couldn't find your hours, menu, or location. They Googled you, got a Yelp page with wrong hours, and went somewhere else. You never knew they existed.

**Talk track:**
- "Your regulars aren't the problem. It's the people who searched 'coffee near me' or 'barbershop Victoria' and ended up at a Yelp page instead of your own site. You're losing people you'll never see in your foot traffic numbers."
- "Try Googling your business right now. Whatever shows up — that's your website. Except you didn't make it, you can't control it, and half the info is probably wrong."
- If they have strong Instagram: "Instagram's great for your existing followers. But nobody searches Instagram for 'falafel Esquimalt.' Google sends them to whoever has a website."

---

## "I can't afford it"

**What they're really saying:** I don't see the ROI, or cash is genuinely tight.

**Your angle:** Separate "can't afford it" from "don't see the value." If it's value — show the math. If it's genuinely tight — offer flexibility. Don't discount your work; restructure the deal.

**Talk track:**
- "Totally fair. Quick question though — how many new customers per month would make $2,500 feel like nothing? For most places it's like... 10? 15? A website that shows up in search will do that."
- "I can split it into two payments if that helps — half upfront, half on delivery. The site pays for itself pretty fast."
- If they push harder: "I get it, cash flow is real. What if we did $1,500 for a clean single-page site — your hours, location, menu, reviews — and you can always add more later?"
- Never go below $1,500. Below that you're volunteering, not running a business.

**Important:** Don't apologize for pricing. $2,500 for a custom-designed site with a real domain is genuinely cheap. Squarespace + a year of hosting + 20 hours of their own time costs more.

## "I don't have time to deal with it"

**What they're really saying:** Every vendor creates work for me and I'm already drowning.

**Your angle:** This is the easiest objection to overcome. You literally already built the thing. Their time investment is near zero.

**Talk track:**
- "That's actually why I built the demo first. I didn't want to schedule a meeting to talk about maybe building something someday. It's already done. Take a look, tell me what to change, and I handle everything."
- "After it's live, you don't have to touch it. No logins to manage, no plugins to update. If you need a change — new hours, new menu item — you text me and I do it."
- "Total time from you: maybe 20 minutes to review the demo and tell me what to tweak. That's it."

---

## "I'll just do it myself on Squarespace / Wix"

**What they're really saying:** I think website builders made you obsolete.

**Your angle:** Don't trash Squarespace. Acknowledge it exists, then show why yours is better *for them specifically.*

**Talk track:**
- "Squarespace is solid if you have a few weekends to learn it. But honestly — when's the last time you had a free weekend? Your time is worth more than mine per hour. Let me do the thing I'm good at so you can do the thing you're good at."
- "The other thing is: Squarespace templates look like Squarespace templates. Your business has a personality. I built something that actually feels like *your* place, not a template with your logo dropped in."
- "Also — Squarespace is $16-33/month. That's $200-400/year, forever. My site is a one-time cost. Two years from now you've already saved money."

**If they say they already started on Squarespace:** "How's that going? [laugh] No seriously, if you're happy with it, no worries. But if it's been sitting half-done for six months, that's kind of my point."

## "Who are you? Why should I trust you?"

**What they're really saying:** Random person emailing me about a website feels scammy.

**Your angle:** Be local, be real, be verifiable. The demo itself is your credibility — you didn't send a sales pitch, you sent finished work.

**Talk track:**
- "I'm Scott, I live here in Victoria. [point to portfolio if in person] I've been designing digital products for about 14 years — used to work at fintech and tech companies, now I'm building sites for local businesses I actually care about."
- "I built you a free demo because I think your business deserves a better web presence than a Yelp listing. If you hate it, no hard feelings."
- "Here are a few other sites I've done for Victoria businesses: [show demos]. You can see the quality. I'm not a template farm."
- If in person: just being there, in their shop, having a normal conversation — that's 80% of the trust battle.

**For email specifically:** Include your real name, your site (scottkbell.com), and your phone number. No "Sent from my agency" — keep it human.

---

## "What happens after? Am I locked in?"

**What they're really saying:** I've been burned by vendors before. Monthly fees, can't leave, hostage situation.

**Your angle:** Full ownership. They get everything. You walk away clean unless they want ongoing help.

**Talk track:**
- "You own everything. The code, the domain, the hosting account — it's all in your name. If you want to take it and never talk to me again, you can. No lock-in, no monthly contract."
- "The only ongoing cost is the domain ($12/year) and hosting ($0-5/month on Cloudflare). That's it. I don't charge monthly fees for the site itself."
- "If you *want* ongoing support — like updating the menu, adding a page, seasonal changes — I offer that for $50/month. But it's optional. You're never stuck."
- "Think of it like buying a painting vs. renting a frame. The painting is yours."

---

## General Principles

1. **Listen more than you pitch.** The best objection handling is asking "what would make a website actually useful for you?" and building from their answer.
2. **The demo does the selling.** Every conversation should loop back to: "just take a look." You built the thing. Let it speak.
3. **Don't be desperate.** You're offering something valuable. If they're not interested, that's fine. Scarcity of desperation builds trust.
4. **Match their energy.** Barbershop owner wants casual. Cafe owner might want thoughtful. Read the room.
5. **Follow up, but don't stalk.** Day 3-5 bump, day 10 final nudge. After that, move on. Some businesses just aren't ready yet.
